### Large potential reduction in economic damages under UN mitigation targets

**[Marshall Burke](https://web.stanford.edu/~mburke/) $\cdot$ Matthew Alampay Davis $\cdot$ [Noah S. Diffenbaugh](https://profiles.stanford.edu/noah-diffenbaugh) (2018)**  
*Nature, 557, 549-553*

In this cross-disciplinary collaboration, we present a probabilistic framework for assessing aggregate economic impacts of anthropogenic warming. Our construction decomposes the wide range of potential outcomes associated with mid-century and end-of-century climate change into distinct sources of uncertainty. These include uncertainties associated with i) econometric estimation of the economic effects of environmental change, ii) climate models of the spatial distribution of anthropogenic warming, iii) the projected schedule of greenhouse gas concentrations associated with a given level of radiative forcing, and iv) the social discounting regime of choice. We apply this framework to quantify the economic benefits of the 2015 Paris Agreement, emphasizing the probable and large harms concentrated in developing countries that would be averted by achieving its most ambitious climate mitigation targets.

### Combining satellite imagery and machine learning to predict poverty

**[Neal Jean](https://nealjean.com/) $\cdot$ [Marshall Burke](https://web.stanford.edu/~mburke/) $\cdot$ [Michael Xie](https://cs.stanford.edu/~eix/) $\cdot$ Matthew Alampay Davis $\cdot$ [David B. Lobell](https://fse.fsi.stanford.edu/people/david_lobell) $\cdot$ [Stefano Ermon](https://cs.stanford.edu/~ermon/) (2016)**  
*Science, 353 (6301), 790-794*  

Lifting methods and public data commonly used in computer vision, a convolutional neural network is primed to identify the features in images most informative of their manually assigned labels (e.g., "dog", "river", "saxophone"). In a 'transfer learning' step, the CNN is then assigned the analogous task of identifying features in unrelated high-resolution daytime satellite imagery most informative of economic development in the photographed area. These abundant time-stamped economic 'labels' are produced automatically by matching georeferenced data on nighttime luminosity, an imprecise but high-frequency proxy with global coverage, critically circumventing need for direct supervision. The resultant 'transfer learning' model is able to assign any input image a low-dimensional vector representation summarizing its activations of the favored features. Finally, a ridge regression model relates these vector representations to high-quality but relatively sparse economic information from nationally representative household surveys, assigning weightings to the selection of image features it determines most relevant for predicting economic development.

Since this pipeline relies only on freely available data and software we have shared freely, we envision this method being particularly valuable as a scalable and cheap supplement to gold-standard data collection efforts in developing-country contexts where they are infrequent or prohibitively expensive. In a first application, we produce disaggregated poverty maps charting estimates of the spatial distribution of consumption expenditure and asset wealth over the entirety of Nigeria, Uganda, Tanzania, Malawi, and Rwanda. Cross-validation analyses demonstrate that our method performs particularly well at distinguishing between even the poorest regions within a country, comparing favorably to expensive alternative methods and notably improving on the input luminosity data, which tends to bottom-code regions well above the poverty line. This indicates our models are able to identify and extract valuable and potentially actionable economic information in daytime satellite imagery which may be relevant to anti-poverty programs where allocating resources by greatest need is a priority and misallocation and search is costly.
